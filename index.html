Modern controls around the ERP you already have.

Replacing an old ERP costs money, retraining, downtime and migration risk, so most companies never do it. Req2PO builds the controls around it instead. Invoices are read, split, checked against the PO and reviewed before anyone enters them. The problems it finds are reported to the right person, tracked, and fixed at the source.

Phase 1

Built in this demo

AP inbox and invoice controls

  • Sorts mail into invoices, statements and general correspondence, and files each to the vendor's folder
  • Splits PDFs that hold several invoices, reads each one, blocks duplicates
  • Compares every invoice with the PO: vendor ID, job, subtotal, tax and freight balances
  • A reviewer verifies the PDFs, a bot enters the invoice, the PM approves
  • Problems become reportable issues with a category, an owner and a feedback loop

First, because only AP changes how it works.

Phase 2

Prototype in this demo

Requisition to PO

  • Project managers fill in one requisition and attach the quote
  • The quote is read and checked against the form
  • Every purchase is declared job, equipment or overhead, which sets the coding
  • Assets over the threshold are capitalized; costs covering a period are carried as prepaid
  • Rules decide, a bot writes the PO, and the ERP assigns the number

Second, because it needs project managers to adopt a new form. It also reduces the problems Phase 1 reports, and extends POs to overhead, which has none today.

Phase 3

Planned, not built

Vendor statement reconciliation

  • Each vendor's statements go to a designated inbox
  • The statement is read and compared with what the ERP shows entered and paid
  • Differences go to a person to review, so nobody reconciles by hand
  • Runs weekly, biweekly or monthly. Biweekly or monthly is recommended

Third. Phase 1 already collects the statements.

How Phase 1 works

1 · InboxMail arrives. It is sorted into invoice, statement, reminder or correspondence and filed to the vendor's folder.
2 · Split and readA PDF with several invoices is split by page, found by the invoice number changing partway through. Vendor, invoice number, date, PO number and amounts are read from each one, which is renamed from what was read and saved.
3 · CompareEach invoice is compared with its PO, which refreshes from the ERP every 5 minutes. Differences are marked.
4 · ReportIf something does not match, or there is no PO at all, the clerk reports it to the PM or purchaser. They assign the PO, put the charges on it, or resolve it another way — with a reason, logged.
5 · ReviewThe reviewer checks the invoice PDF and the PO, then sends it to the bot.
6 · EnterThe bot enters it, re-checks the balance first, and reads back what it entered.
7 · ApproveThe PM sees the voucher with the PDF attached and approves or rejects it.

Checks and balances

1 · Automated checks15 checks per invoice. Each failed check is a reportable issue with a category.
2 · A person looksThe reviewer compares the invoice PDF with the PO before anything is entered.
3 · The bot verifiesIt re-checks the balance at entry, reads back the voucher, and voids it if anything differs.
4 · The PM approvesThe voucher arrives with the PDF attached. A rejection voids it and restores the PO.
5 · Everything is loggedEvery action has an actor and a time. The issue report shows where the process breaks.
  • Separation of duties: the invoice team can report and process. Only the project manager or original purchaser can change a PO, assign one to an invoice that arrived without it, or accept a difference — each with a reason, a sign-off and a log entry. Switch the viewer to AP clerk and try it: the system refuses.
  • Duplicates are blocked on the invoice number, normalized first so HW-30418 and HW30418 are read as the same invoice, across invoices already entered and invoices received but not yet entered. Manual entries are allowed, marked as manual, and checked the same way.

Coding what has no job to code it to

A job purchase codes itself: the job and cost code decide the GL account. Overhead, SG&A and equipment have no such structure, so today they are categorized by whoever happens to key the invoice. Phase 2 moves that decision to the requisition, before the purchase.

  • Three classes, declared up front. Job related, equipment, or overhead and operating expense. The class decides whether the cost hits a job or the general ledger.
  • A list that covers the ordinary cases. Materials, subcontract, rental, office supplies, repairs, software, insurance, professional fees, utilities — each carrying its GL account. "Other" is available, and has to be explained in writing.
  • Capitalize or expense. An asset at or above the $2,500 threshold is coded to fixed assets and depreciated over its useful life, not expensed this period.
  • Expense or prepay. A cost covering several months is carried to prepaid expenses and amortized across the periods it benefits, instead of overstating one month and understating the rest.

The system proposes the class, the account and the treatment from the purchase type, the amount and the period it covers. A person confirms it. Overhead coding is never auto-approved — the review is the control, and deciding it at the requisition means the treatment is settled before the PO exists rather than argued about at close.

What an invoice stuck in the inbox actually costs

An invoice that cannot be entered is not a late payment. It is an unrecorded liability, and the damage spreads well past accounts payable.

  • Cost to date is understated. Costs that belong to a job are not on it, so every figure built on cost to date is wrong.
  • Percent complete falls. Under cost-to-cost, percent complete is cost incurred over total estimated cost. Missing cost means a lower percentage than the job has really reached.
  • Revenue is under-recognized. A lower percent complete recognizes less revenue this period, and less gross profit with it on a profitable job. It reverses when the invoices land, so margin swings between periods on nothing but paperwork.
  • Overbillings are overstated. With revenue under-recognized, billings in excess of costs and estimated earnings is larger than it should be, and the balance sheet carries a liability that is not really there.
  • The close slips. The controller waits while somebody chases a PO, and the accrual is a guess until they stop chasing.
  • The job cost report misleads the other way. It shows the job underrunning its budget, because the costs simply are not there yet. Nobody investigates a job that looks like it is coming in under.
  • Reporting inherits it. The CFO reports figures that are not complete, and the correction lands in a later period as a journal entry.
  • Payments go late. Fees, credit holds, and a vendor relationship spent on something avoidable.
  • Reconciliation absorbs the backlog. A payment forces a pile of invoices in at once, which is where keying errors come from.

This is why the checks run at intake rather than at the end. Catching it here protects the completeness of the books, not just the AP queue.

Why a layer instead of a replacement

  • The ERP stays the book of record. No migration, no data move, no parallel run.
  • Nobody retrains. Accountants keep the screens they know. Project managers get one simple form.
  • It works with a closed system. Everything goes through one small adapter, so the ERP never needs plug-ins or integrations of its own.
  • It fixes the expensive part first. Most AP rework starts before the invoice: no PO, wrong job, wrong cost code, wrong vendor, tax errors.
  • It can be swapped later. If the company ever changes ERPs, the requisition and invoice logic moves with it. Only the adapter changes.

Built on a construction model, but the shape is general

Every organization that buys things has the same three problems: purchases made without authorization, invoices arriving with nothing to match them against, and statements nobody reconciles. Accounts payable, purchase requisitions and reconciliation are not construction tools. What differs between industries is one thing: the dimension a cost is coded to.

  • Construction codes to a job and cost code.
  • A law firm codes to a client and matter.
  • A nonprofit codes to a grant or program, where the restriction matters more than the budget.
  • Healthcare codes to a location or service line.
  • Professional services code to an engagement.
  • An organization with no projects at all codes to a department and GL account — which is exactly what the overhead path in Phase 2 already does.

The checks do not change. Is the vendor known. Is there an authorization to match against. Is there enough left on it. Is the tax right. Is this a duplicate. Does the math add up. Has someone with the authority approved the exception, and is it written down. Only the name of the dimension changes — and in this build that sits behind one adapter, which is the point of putting the logic outside the ERP in the first place.

What replacing the ERP would involve

Third-party estimates for small and mid-size companies, from consultant and vendor guides rather than independent surveys. Real quotes vary widely, and none of these figures are specific to one company's system.

Money

  • Total first-year cost is commonly quoted at about $25,000 to $150,000 for small businesses and $100,000 to $750,000 for mid-market firms.
  • The software license is only about 15% to 25% of that. Services are often $1 to $1.50 for every software dollar.
  • Data migration is budgeted at roughly 5% to 20% of the project, and messy data adds more. Moving 8 or more years of history can pass $75,000 on its own.
  • Training for 10 to 30 users is quoted at $5,000 to $15,000 over 2 to 4 weeks.
  • One industry study found more than 80% of data migration projects run over time or budget, by about 30% on average.
  • Running the old and new systems side by side means paying for both for weeks or months.

Changes to the infrastructure around it

  • On-premise: servers, network hardware, operating system, database, storage and backup. Cloud: a subscription instead, plus a network check for bandwidth, latency, VPN and firewall readiness.
  • Every integration has to be listed and rebuilt. Mid-market firms often have 15 to 50.
  • Custom reports and any changes made directly to the database must be reimplemented.
  • Decide how many years of history to move. Most companies need 2 to 3, and archive the rest.
  • Pick the cutover date to avoid quarter-end and billing cycles.

What the layer costs instead

Not nothing. Someone has to build and maintain the ERP connection, host the tool, pay for document reading, and review exceptions. If the connection works by automating screens, an ERP update can break it. But there is no data migration, no retraining, and it can be rolled out one process at a time.

What it will do

  1. Each vendor's statements arrive in a designated inbox for that vendor.
  2. The statement is read: each invoice, credit and payment it lists.
  3. It is compared with what the ERP shows entered and paid for that vendor.
