# Video script — about 3 minutes 57 seconds

**On length.** At 140 words a minute this reads in 3:57. Most people present nearer 155, which lands it around 3:35 — but that leaves little margin, so record Q2 first and time it. If it runs past 95 seconds, drop the two sentences marked *(cut if long)* below; the script still works without them.

Record in four takes, one per question, and join them. If a take runs long, cut a sentence rather than speed up — rushing reads as nerves.

---

>
> I came to accounting for the part most people find tedious: the reconciliation where something doesn't tie, and you have to work out why.

## Q2 — Walk through your project (90 seconds, ~211 words)

> This is Req2PO. The first phase is in production at my company now, and we take three to five hundred invoices a week.
>
> In construction, the people placing orders aren't accountants. So invoices arrive with no PO number, the wrong job, or tax on a tax-exempt job.
>
> And an invoice that can't be entered isn't a late payment — it's an unrecorded liability. Job costs are understated, the close slips, and the CFO's numbers aren't complete.
>
> Our ERP is old and closed, so I built the controls around it instead of replacing it. What you're seeing runs on made-up data; the real one runs on my employer's.

## Q3 — How AI reshapes your target role (87 seconds, ~204 words)

> I don't think AI replaces accountants. It moves where the judgement goes.
>
> Most of accounts payable isn't judgement. It's reading a document, finding the PO, checking the math, the tax, the duplicates. A model can read. Rules can check. So the mechanical errors go, and the data entry with them. What's left is the part that needs an accountant: deciding how something should be treated.
>
> So I was careful about what the AI decides, which is nothing. AI reads. Rules decide. A bot enters. A person reviews. Every check is a rule an auditor can read — "the model thought it was fine" is not an audit trail.
>
> *(cut if long: the audit-trail sentence)*
>
> It also counts its own failures. Every problem gets a category. So I can tell you with a number, not a feeling, that about eighty percent of our invoices arrive with something that has to be resolved — and which issues.
>
> That's not an accounts payable problem, it's a purchasing problem — and it shows up in the financial statements. Finding that is the controller's job, which is what I'm working toward.
>
> The work gets simpler to do and more demanding to think about — which is a training question as much as a software one.

## Q4 — The broader opportunity (35 seconds, ~81 words)

> The opportunity nobody funds is small and mid-size companies on closed, old systems. The advice is to replace the ERP. They can't afford it, so they do nothing and keep paying in rework.
>
> You don't have to replace the system to put modern controls around it. The ERP stays the book of record, nobody retrains, and the error is caught before the entry instead of corrected after.
>
> That's what I want to keep building. That's why this scholarship matters to me.

## Before you record

- Open the demo link in a private browsing window and confirm it loads.
- Process the inbox before you start, so you're not waiting on screen.
- Say the ERP is old and closed. **Don't name it, and don't name your employer.**
- Screen-share **only the demo**. No real invoices, vendors, screens or documents from work.
- You can say Phase 1 is in production, give the weekly volume and the eighty percent. Keep it at that level — the rules, prompts and screens of the real build belong to your employer.
- Say plainly that the demo runs on a mock system with fictional data. Honest scope is one of the things the council says it's looking for.
